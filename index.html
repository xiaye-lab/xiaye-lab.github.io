    Beyond carbon: a systematic review of building decarbonization co-benefits and quantification methods

    Ye Xia, Tao Wang, Yu Qian Ang · Sustainable Cities and Society, 149, 107774 (2026)

    Systematic reviewCo-benefitsHealth
    DOI
    Decarbonizing buildings is critical for urban sustainability and climate mitigation. However, mitigation strategies are frequently evaluated solely through carbon metrics, leaving broader co-benefits insufficiently synthesized. This (narrow) framing obscures the full value proposition of building decarbonization interventions and impedes their practical adoption. This systematic review analyzes a decade of literature (January 2016 to December 2025), screening 2,183 records from Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed. We present a framework linking supply- and demand-side decarbonization strategies to 18 distinct co-benefits across four domains, assessed at the building, technology, and policy levels. Geographic disparities are pronounced—China and the United States account for nearly half of all studies, while the Global South is underrepresented. Environmental (60.0%) and economic (61.2%) co-benefits are most frequently reported, whereas health (34.1%) and societal impacts (24.7%) appear less often. Quantification methods vary by level of analysis. Building-level studies (n=46) rely primarily on physics-based simulations and report 1.63 co-benefits per study on average. Policy- and technology-level studies (n=39) typically apply integrated energy–economy–climate models, with 82.1% reporting at least two co-benefit domains, averaging 2.49 co-benefits per study—likely reflecting the structural capacity of integrated frameworks to generate multi-domain outputs. Reductions in outdoor air pollution are most frequently co-reported alongside reduced mortality and lower healthcare expenditures, suggesting a strong thematic association across environmental, health, and economic outcomes. Overall, the literature reports substantial co-benefits from net-zero building strategies, which are also a pathway to improve public health, economic resilience, and social equity.

    Urban morphology as a driver of urban sustainability trade-offs and synergies: an explainable graph neural network analysis of Singapore

    Xia, Y., et al. · Sustainable Cities and Society (2026)

    GNNGNNExplainerSingapore
    DOI
    Urban development in high-density cities must balance economic growth, environmental performance, and social well-being, yet how urban form mediates the coupling between these dimensions remains poorly quantified and seldom interpretable. This study introduces a data-driven framework for diagnosing and optimizing the environmental and socio-economic performance of dense urban environments. We adopt the CRITIC-Entropy method to construct two composite metrics—the Environmental Cost and Stress Index (ECSI) and the Socio-Economic Vitality Index (SEVI)—and apply them to Singapore as a high-density urban testbed. Using 15 key urban morphological parameters, we train a Graph Neural Network (GNN) and employ explainable AI (XAI), specifically GNNExplainer coupled with Partial Dependence Plots (PDP), to uncover the non-linear and spatially embedded relationships between urban morphology and these (eco-socio-economic) performance outcomes. The framework identifies four Pareto-frontier archetypes that characterize distinct morphology–performance pathways. Distance to the central business district, building coverage ratio, and greenery coverage ratio are the most dominant drivers of environmental cost, while building-height diversity, land-cover diversity, and sky view factor most strongly shape socio-economic vitality. Across the 15 indicators, most relationships are synergistic or mixed, with genuine trade-offs concentrated in a small set of dominant drivers, notably building coverage ratio and distance to the central business district. Central districts generally exhibit the strongest spillover benefits for both dimensions (ECSI and SEVI) relative to peripheral areas. These results provide interpretable and actionable benchmarks for site-specific retrofitting and targeted urban regeneration in high-density cities.

    Balancing urban environmental and societal performance in high-density cities: Morphological trade-offs and optimization strategies in Shenzhen and Hong Kong

    Xia, Y., et al. · Sustainable Cities and Society (2026)

    XGBoost + SHAPParetoShenzhen · Hong Kong
    DOI
    Achieving both environmental sustainability and societal prosperity in high-density cities remains a major planning challenge because the same urban form can improve one dimension while worsening the other. This study examines how urban form and land use shape these trade-offs in Shenzhen and Hong Kong, two compact but developmentally distinct cities in southern China. Using a 500 m grid, we measure environmental performance (EPI) from land surface temperature and carbon dioxide emissions, and societal performance (SPI) from night-time light intensity and population density. We then combine XGBoost and SHAP to identify the morphological features that most strongly influence EPI, SPI, and their combined eco-societal performance (ESPI). Three findings emerge. First, neighborhood-scale morphology plays a more consistent role than purely on-site form in shaping both environmental and societal performance, indicating that a block’s outcomes are strongly conditioned by the urban fabric in which it is embedded. Second, the dominant morphological constraint differs across the two cities. In Shenzhen, surrounding sky openness and building enclosure exert the strongest aggregate influence, whereas in Hong Kong, floor area ratio remains the decisive net driver, suggesting that the two cities require different morphological priorities for balancing environmental and societal goals. Third, Pareto-front analysis identifies mid-rise, mid-density blocks as the most reliably balanced typology, and counterfactual simulations on selected underperforming sites show that targeted increases in surrounding greenery and calibrated height adjustments can improve SPI by as much as 94% or EPI by up to 45% without necessarily sacrificing the other dimension. These findings show that improving sustainability in compact cities requires city-specific, neighborhood-scale interventions rather than one-size-fits-all densification or greening strategies. By linking morphological trade-offs with optimization-oriented planning guidance, this study provides an interpretable framework for identifying renewal priorities and reconciling environmental and societal objectives in high-density urban settings.

    The optimal buffer for urban features to impact outdoor thermal comfort

    Ye Xia, Weisheng Lu, Ziyu Peng, Jianlei Niu · Sustainable Cities and Society, 131, 106733 (2025)

    CatBoost + SHAPLCZHong Kong
    DOI
    This paper aims to answer one question: Within which buffer, and in what ways, do urban features impact outdoor thermal comfort (OTC) across diverse urban settings most effectively? To address this question, the research adopts a buffer-dependent workflow consisting of five steps: (1) Cooler Area Searching; (2) OTC Simulation; (3) Cross-scale Validation; (4) Cooler Area Identification; and (5) Space Design Analysis. At the methodology's core is a series of machine learning tools (e.g., Bayesian-optimized CatBoost models and Shapley Additive exPlanations) used to identify the optimal buffer and interpret the urban features that matter. The results showed that an optimal buffer range of 30 to 50 m accurately captures the thermal impacts of urban features across eight typical local climate zones (LCZs). Notably, urban features’ critical impact on OTC was highlighted, including percentage of view (PV), orientation of view (OV), average height index (AHI), and vegetation coverage (VE). For the first time, this research articulates the optimal buffer for urban features to maximize OTC in different LCZs. The findings provide buffer-sensitive microclimates interventions and content-specific strategies for urban designers and policymakers to address the rampant threat of heat waves and foster sustainable development.

    Understanding the impacts of space design on local outdoor thermal comfort: An approach combining DepthmapX and XGBoost

    Ye Xia, Weisheng Lu, Ziyu Peng, Jinfeng Lou, Jianxiang Huang, Jianlei Niu · Energy and Buildings, 332, 115451 (2025)

    Space syntaxXGBoostHong Kong
    DOI
    Researchers and designers alike are keen to explore proper space designs to alleviate the heat waves. A niche research area of this type focuses on the smaller scale, local communities to achieve the so-called ‘cool spots’ for residents. However, despite the momentum gained so far, it remains unclear what the key design elements are (e.g., space orientation, aspect ratio, or building density) and how they interact with each other in impacting outdoor thermal comfort (OTC), particularly in some complex, vertical ‘spots’. This research aims to provide an improved understanding of cool spot reasoning by proposing a new paradigm to engage DepthmapX (an analytic tool for urban spatial configuration), XGBoost (a machine learning tool), and on-site verification. By implementing the paradigm on a university campus in Hong Kong for the case study, it was discovered that Percentage of View (PV), Average Height Index (AHI), and Connectivity (CON) are the three most influential factors leading to the formation of a ‘cool spot’. DepthmapX can not only help quantify space designs but also help translate the indexes back to real-life space design options. The XGBoost can help better interpret the pathway from different space design indexes to different OTC but more explainable causal relationships are desired. This research advanced our understanding of the impacts of different space designs on OTC and provided references to designers in achieving OTC in smaller--scale, local communities. It also opens a new avenue to understand the causal relationships in a more detailed and explainable fashion.
